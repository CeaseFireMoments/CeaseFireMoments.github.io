Rediscovering Jonah

#GateWisdom #TimothyKeller #J

Author: Timothy Keller

Type of Media: Book

Description:

Previously published as The Prodigal Prophet.

The story of Jonah is one of the most well-known parables in the Bible. It is also the most misunderstood. Many people, even those who are nonreligious, are familiar with Jonah: A rebellious prophet who defies God and is swallowed by a whale. But there’s much more to Jonah’s story than most of us realize.

In The Prodigal Prophet, pastor and New York Times bestselling author Timothy Keller reveals the hidden depths within the book of Jonah. Keller makes the case that Jonah was one of the worst prophets in the entire Bible. And yet there are unmistakably clear connections between Jonah, the prodigal son, and Jesus. Jesus in fact saw himself in Jonah. How could one of the most defiant and disobedient prophets in the Bible be compared to Jesus?

Jonah’s journey also doesn’t end when he is freed from the belly of the fish. There is an entire second half to his story–but it is left unresolved within the text of the Bible. Why does the book of Jonah end on what is essentially a cliffhanger? In these pages, Timothy Keller provides an answer to the extraordinary conclusion of this biblical parable–and shares the powerful Christian message at the heart of Jonah’s story. - see overview link for source

Setting

Things to explore before diving into this book.

  • Jonah
    • From the House of Israel
      • In the time frame of this story - Israel is prosperous and Nineveh is not. Assyria had suffered a series of military defeats and they were also dealing with famine additionally during the same time a major earthquake happened as did a solar eclipse.
    • The Scroll of Jonah
  • Gentile
    • Anyone who is not in covenant with YHWH, even if they are a native born Israelite.
  • Assyria and Nineveh (House of the Fish)
    • Assyria was struck by a series of massive disasters, plaques, solar eclipse, civil revolts which were terrifying omens of imminent divine wrath.
    • Ancient Mesopotamian texts show that when these events occurred, the king and his people would dress in sackcloth, fast, and declare emergency acts of penitence to appease the gods
    • Around this same time, Jonah walks into Nineveh and says, "In forty days Nineveh will be overturned." Due to the recent events, the people and the king were primed to instantly believe Jonah's warning and repent. And following ancient practices, their response would be to dress in sackcloth and fast.
  • evil
    • ra'ah (רָעָה) can be translated as evil, wickedness, calamity, distress, harm, trouble. It can mean a moral evil (wrongdoing), and also disaster/calamity that befalls someone, making it a term that can describe both the wrong done and the harm suffered
  • Jeroboam II
    • The king successfully seized vital trade routes and extracted heavy tribute from conquered territories like Damascus. This brought an influx of luxury goods—such as ivory, fine wine, and rare spices—into the capital. The wealthy elite, aristocrats, and merchants viewed the restoration of Israel's borders as a sign of permanent security and divine favor.
    • Jonah served as a bearer of good news for Jeroboam’s administration. He delivered a prophecy predicting that Jeroboam II would successfully expand Israel's borders and reclaim lost territory. History shows this came true, bringing massive wealth to the upper classes.
      • The text explicitly states that Jeroboam II "did evil in the eyes of the Lord". Yet, Jonah still willingly delivered a message of victory to him. This points to Jonah's specific theological view.
      • Jonah is called to Nineveh (the capital of Assyria) - the very empire Amos and Hosea will later prophesy will crush Israel.
    • For the average citizen and the lower class, they viewed the era as a time of cruel systemic injustice.
    • The prophets of the era—most notably Amos and Hosea—viewed Jeroboam II's reign with utter dread, condemning it as an age of moral degeneracy and spiritual bankruptcy.
      • Amos essentially took Jonah's prophecy and reversed it, warning the people that the very land Jonah promised they would win would soon be stripped away due to their corruption.
        • In Amos 6:13-14, Amos confronts the nation for gloating over territorial victories. Amos specifically declares that God will raise up a nation (Assyria) to crush Israel from Lebo-hamath to the valley of the Arabah (the Dead Sea area).
      • Hosea undid the false sense of divine endorsement that Jonah’s military prophecy helped create.
        • Hosea is screaming at God's chosen people to repent. Hosea explicitly warns that because Israel refuses to repent, they will be conquered and exiled by Assyria (Hosea 9:3; 11:5)

Chapter: Prodigal Prophet

Both episodes show how Jonah, a staunch religious believer, regards and relates to people who are racially and religiously different from him.

His anger's trigger is God's action which he clearly stated in Chapter 4: "God relented of the disaster... it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was angry" (3:10-4:1) — the reaction stems from what God did.

  • Matthew 20:1-16. The workers grumble at the landowner's generosity, and he asks, "Is your eye evil because I am good?"

Like many throughout the bible, Jonah wants to decide what is tov and ra (good and bad) instead of letting Yah decided. When it's tov to eat the fruit and when it is ra to eat it. When mercy is tov and when mercy is ra. The message of Jonah is the same as everywhere else in Scripture. He sets himself up in the place of Yah. He doesn't trust his god; he wants control.

  • Religious means relating to religion, having a strong belief in a god or gods, or showing strict devotion to a practice. Having deep faith and actively following the rules and customs of a religion.
  • Jonah sincerely loved God and was a devoted long experienced god-appointed prophet. But he had a knee jerk but deeply felt averse reaction to God's order that is so very human. Yet it also leaves us with how he could entertain rebellion as a legitimate possibility, let alone follow through with it to the death?
  • Isaiah 5:20 ("woe to those who call evil good and good evil") and Isaiah 55:8-9
  • Jonah may have hated the Ninevites, but Jonah 4:2 roots his objectionin his prior knowledge of YHWH's revealed character from Sinai ("I knew that you are a gracious God, and merciful...") — the text locates his resistance in theological, not an unrecorded personal grievance.
    • Chus (חוּס) — "to pity, look upon with compassion, spare" — used twice in the closing verses: "you have had pity (chastah) on the vine... shouldn't I have pity (achus) on Nineveh" (Jonah 4:10-11) — this is the term YHWH uses to frame the entire controversy, and it is a term of sparing/compassion, not the specific vocabulary of "forgiveness" (which in Hebrew would typically be salach, סָלַח, "to forgive, pardon" — not used anywhere in Jonah). The book's own explanation for Jonah's flight and anger is entirely theological, stated in his own words (Jonah 4:2) — not racial, political, tribal, or biographical.
    • Jeremiah 18:7-10. God announces judgment on a nation, and if it turns, he relents. This explains why Jonah's "overturned in forty days" wasn't a false prophecy, and Jonah knew it (see Deut 18:22).
  • Jesus contrasts the Jews of his generation with the Gentiles of Nineveh (Matt 12:41): those who think themselves God's chosen are worse than the people Jonah warned, and Yeshua is greater than Jonah, whom all Jews revered.

If Nineveh is the basis of the problem, the difference is covenantal not racial. Israel is a "holy nation" (goy qadosh, Exodus 19:6), set apart by covenant relationship with YHWH; all other nations (Gentiles/goyim) stand outside that specific covenant relationship, regardless of shared ancestry through Noah.

  • YHWH himself frequently presents fundamental distinctions:
    • In Abraham's time it was the Hebrews and everybody else.
    • In Jacob's time it was the Israelites and everybody else.
      • Israel — God's covenant people (this includes native born and converts)
      • The nations — the other peoples/Gentiles (this includes native born Israelites who were non covenant peoples)
    • Jeremiah 10 - Hear the word which Yahweh speaks to you, house of Israel! 2 Yahweh says, “Don’t learn the way of the nations, and don’t be dismayed at the signs of the sky; for the nations are dismayed at them. ... Don’t be afraid of them; for they can’t do evil, neither is it in them to do good.” ... For Yahweh says, “Behold, I will sling out the inhabitants of the land at this time, and will distress them, that they may feel it.”
      • He is standing in the exact spot the religious leaders at Jesus's time were. The "Jews" who were leaders of Judaism. A native born "Jew" who was not a part of Judaism was called a gentile. And a non native born "Jew" could convert to Judaism and would be called a Jew.
    • Jeremiah 9: 25 “Behold, the days come,” says Yahweh, “that I will punish all those who are circumcised only in their flesh: 26 Egypt, Judah, Edom, the children of Ammon, Moab, and all who have the corners of their hair cut off, who dwell in the wilderness, for all the nations are uncircumcised, and all the house of Israel are uncircumcised in heart.”
  • God set Israel apart from the peoples: Deut 7:6; 14:2; Lev 20:24-26; Ps 147:19-20; John 4:22; Rom 3:1-2; 9:4-5. Order, not exclusion: "to the Jew first and also to the Greek" (Rom 1:16), and Gentiles are grafted into the same tree (Rom 11:16-24). Preference brings greater accountability: Amos 3:2; Matt 11:20-24; Luke 12:47-48.
  • The door was always open to "the multitude" who follow him:
      • A "mixed multitude" left Egypt with Israel (Exod 12:38); one law for native-born and sojourner (Exod 12:48-49; Num 9:14; 15:15-16; Lev 24:22); the sojourner gets an inheritance among the tribes (Ezek 47:22-23); Solomon asks God to answer the foreigner who prays toward the temple (1 Kings 8:41-43); Isa 56:3-8.
    • Covenant is by allegiance, not descent: Matt 3:9; John 8:39-44; Rom 9:6-8.

The Bible never categorizes by race - it categorizes peoples by lineage/nation, most explicitly in the Table of Nations (Genesis 10)

  • Race is a social and political category used to group people based on perceived physical traits, such as skin color, facial features, and hair texture
    • "Using our modern, physical definition of race (based largely on skin color and continental ancestry), there would have been very little to no racial difference between Jonah, the sailors, and the Ninevites."
  • Jonah is a Hebrew (Jonah 1:9), of the tribe of Zebulun, from Gath Hepher (2 Kings 14:25; Joshua 19:10-13)
    • Hebrews/Israelites trace lineage through Shem, son of Noah — "the children of Shem" (Genesis 10:21-31
  • The Ninevites trace lineage through Ham ("Cush became the father of Nimrod... out of that land he went out into Assyria, and built Nineveh" (Genesis 10:8-11, WEB)
    • Nineveh was captial of the Assyrian empire. Asshur (Assyria's namesake) is separately listed as a son of Shem: "the children of Shem: Elam, Asshur, Arpachshad, Lud, and Aram" (Genesis 10:22, WEB)
    • By the time of the historical Assyrian empire (including Jonah's Nineveh), Assyrians are generally regarded as a Semitic-language people in extrabiblical/linguistic terms, consistent with the Shemitic listing in Genesis 10:22 Assyria was one of the cruelest and most violent empires of ancient times.
  • Israel's own relationship to YHWH's chesed and rachum is grounded in an established covenant (Exodus 34 is itself part of a covenant-renewal scene, following the golden calf).   • other nations were apportioned to "sons of God" — bene elohim (בְּנֵי אֱלֹהִים) — implying each nation had its own supernatural ruler (Deuteronomy 32:8–9, "according to the number of the sons of God")
